RollTrackApp started as my UC Irvine capstone at the 2025 ICS Project Expo and became a company. The product runs the parts of a martial arts gym that nobody opens a browser for fun to look at: attendance, payments, student records, gamification, and retention analytics. “Taking the pulse of your gym” is the short version. The longer version is that a gym owner should be able to answer three questions in one screen — who is training, who is paying, and who is drifting away.

> “The expo kind of became my career.”
>
> — Christopher Tapia, [UC Irvine ICS](https://ics.uci.edu/2026/03/19/from-project-expo-to-cto-alumnus-christopher-tapia-kicked-his-career-into-high-gear/)

<h2 id="the-product">The product</h2>

The web client is Next.js. The application API is Django, and the primary database is PostgreSQL. That split is deliberate: the client owns the interaction model, the API owns the rules, and the database owns the truth. A gym can change a membership plan, mark attendance, take a payment, or adjust a belt requirement without the other two layers guessing what happened.

Attendance is the spine. Every check-in is an event with a student, a class, a timestamp, and a location. Payments attach to a student and a plan. Gamification reads the attendance stream and turns milestones into something a student can see. Retention analytics reads the same stream and turns it into something an owner can act on. One event model feeding three surfaces is the difference between a product and three disconnected features.

<h2 id="retention-analytics">Retention analytics</h2>

Retention is the number every gym owner asks about, and it is also the easiest number to fake. I did not want a dashboard that printed a churn percentage with no definition behind it. The analytics path is built around a simple rule: a student is at risk when their recent attendance cadence drops relative to their own history, not relative to a gym-wide average that hides the individual.

That means pre-aggregating attendance into windows the API can read cheaply, then comparing a student's recent window to their own baseline. The heavy lifting happens in PostgreSQL, not in the browser, and not in a request that scans every check-in the gym has ever recorded. The result is a list an owner can work through: who has not been in for a while, who changed their weekly rhythm, and who is still coming but paying for a plan they no longer use.

I am not going to invent a churn-reduction percentage for a marketing page. The honest claim is structural: the analytics exist, they are fed by the same attendance events the front desk already creates, and they are designed to be fast enough to open on a phone between classes.

<h2 id="migration">AWS → DigitalOcean migration</h2>

The move was cost-driven. The workload was predictable, the team was small, and the AWS bill was sized for a company we were not. RollTrack now runs on DigitalOcean, and the cutover happened without customer-visible downtime: PostgreSQL restored cleanly, the schema matched, and the application connected on the first try.

What changed beyond the invoice is the operational surface — one documented place to change a hostname, one deploy path, and a runbook that covers the worker processes, the cron jobs, and the payment callbacks instead of only the web request path. That is the durable outcome of a migration: a shorter list of assumptions.
